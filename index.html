Open, evidence-led guidance for business decisions

From AI output to business value.

AI can produce remarkably complete-looking work. The useful management question is not how impressive the output looks, but which claim the next decision requires and what evidence actually establishes it.

Four distinctions worth remembering

Access is not operating capability. A licence, API, model, or agent proves access—not that the organisation can repeatedly use, verify, operate, and support it for a particular job.
Output is not completion. Generation may be only one part of specification, evaluation, integration, delivery, and support.
Judgement is not authority. A human or AI may evaluate options; permission to act and accountability for the outcome are separate questions.
Activity is not value. More prompts, tokens, generated code, licences, meetings, or travel are not outcomes by themselves.

Decision first

Require the weakest claim sufficient for the decision.

Do not average the six claims into a readiness percentage. Strength at Access or Output cannot compensate for a missing decision-critical Deliverable, Operating capability, Outcome, or Value claim.

Typical decision threshold — adjust for consequence, reversibility, regulation, contract, and context.
Decision soughtClaim that normally matters
Get access to try it?01 Access — the tool is named, the people who would try it can use it, and its terms allow the trial.
Keep exploring?02 Output — enough to reproduce or inspect the artefact/action and learn from it.
May someone rely on it?03 Deliverable — fit for the named use against explicit acceptance criteria.
May we sell, operate, or support it repeatedly?04 Operating capability — ownership, controls, fallback, recovery, maintenance, and support exist.
Did it change the result?05 Outcome — the named measure changed against a comparable baseline or counterfactual.
Should we scale, renew, expand, or stop?06 Value — the outcome is worth the full relevant cost, risk, alternatives, and trade-offs.

An Access decision only justifies trying the tool. Having access never licenses relying on what it produces, operating it, or claiming an outcome; each of those needs its own decision and a higher claim.

Access → Output → Deliverable → Operating capability → Outcome → Value

The arrows are a memory aid, not six mandatory lifecycle stages. A prototype may legitimately stop at Output; a cheap experiment can create an Outcome by removing uncertainty; a stronger claim is required only when the decision requires it.

Use this in a meeting

Eight questions before the next decision.

These questions apply to human, AI, automated, and hybrid work. The actor can change; the evidentiary standard for the same decision does not.

  1. 1

    What exactly have we demonstrated?
    Separate a working demo from assumptions about readiness, adoption, or demand.

  2. 2

    What did the AI know, and what did it infer?
    Make supplied context and generated assumptions visible.

  3. 3

    What remains before the intended use?
    Name unresolved work, acceptance criteria, and decisions.

  4. 4

    What work disappeared or moved, and where does the bottleneck go next?
    Name what disappeared, what moved and the next bottleneck; look beyond local generation speed to the end-to-end result.

  5. 5

    Which actor or combination performs this task or decision best?
    Compare human, AI, automated, and hybrid processes rather than assuming the answer.

  6. 6

    Who decides, who is accountable, and who verifies, runs and supports it?
    Name who holds authority, accountability, approval, verification, operation and support, and where recourse sits.

  7. 7

    Which business outcome are we trying to change?
    Include learning or uncertainty removed when that is the purpose.

  8. 8

    What evidence would justify the next decision, and when should we stop?
    Define what would change the answer before adding more process.
